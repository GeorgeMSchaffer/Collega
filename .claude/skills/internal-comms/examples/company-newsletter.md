## When to use

A recurring roundup for a **whole-company** audience — most readers will not share your context and
have no stake in the details. That constraint drives every choice below.

For an audience inside the work, use `3p-updates.md`; it can afford specifics this cannot.

## Structure

- **A subject line that says the most interesting true thing.** Not "September Update".
- **One lead item**, given real room. A newsletter with six equal items has no lead and is read as
  six things to skip.
- **Short items after it**, each two or three sentences, each ending in a link for the people who
  want depth.
- **A closing section for the mechanically useful** — dates, joiners, deadlines, where to ask.

## Writing for a whole-company audience

- **Translate, don't summarise.** Shortening a technical item leaves technical writing that is
  merely shorter. Say what changed for a person: *"expense reports now approve in a day instead of a
  week"*, not *"the approval workflow was refactored"*.
- **Expand every acronym on first use**, every issue, including the ones that feel universal. The
  reader who does not know it is exactly the reader you are writing for.
- **Name people who did the work**, with their consent. This is the part readers remember.
- **One number per item, at most.** Whole-company readers do not retain tables.
- **Do not bury bad news, and do not lead with it either.** Give it its own item, plainly worded,
  in the body.

## Tone

Warm, specific, unhurried. The failure mode is corporate cheerfulness — exclamation marks doing the
work that a concrete detail should do. One vivid true detail beats a paragraph of enthusiasm.

## Anti-patterns

| Don't | Do |
|---|---|
| "September Update" | A subject line with the news in it. |
| Six items of equal weight | One lead, then short items. |
| Unexpanded acronyms | Expand on first use, every issue. |
| "The team has been hard at work…" | Say what they shipped. |
| Hiding a cancellation or a departure | Give it its own item. |
